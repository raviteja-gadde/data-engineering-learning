The Central Thesis

"A human hesitates at data that looks wrong; an agent acts on it anyway."

For three decades, data systems were built for human analysts who supply implicit context, judgment, and skepticism. Autonomous agents provide none of this. When agents become primary data consumers, your data architecture becomes your AI architecture.

Sadalage and Chandrasekaran's argument is structural, not incremental: you cannot make data "AI-ready" by adding a RAG layer on top of existing infrastructure. The implicit work humans performed—sniffing out bad data, knowing what "revenue" means, judging when to hesitate—must be engineered into the data layer itself. Skip that, and agents will confidently propagate errors at machine speed.

The article identifies four interdependent layers that must be built bottom-up—data contracts and quality, traceability and governance, the context layer, and agent-ready data access—with observability running alongside all of them from day one.

The Behavioral Gap

Human analysts brought enormous implicit knowledge to their work. They understood organizational context, noticed suspicious patterns, and paused at questionable data. Agents lack all of this. The gap isn't a bug to patch—it's a fundamental characteristic that reshapes what "data quality" must mean.

Human Analyst

  • Knows which tables to trust and which to avoid
  • Notices round numbers, holiday dates, suspicious prices
  • Understands "revenue" means net after returns in this org
  • Pauses when data "smells wrong"
  • Reads dashboards, then acts manually
  • Can explain decisions after the fact

Autonomous Agent

  • Treats every data source equally—no trust ranking
  • Acts confidently on stale, wrong, or suspicious values
  • Has no institutional knowledge of metric definitions
  • Cannot "smell" bad data; errors cascade silently
  • Must perform actions directly on systems
  • Reasoning must be captured as decisions happen
The Confidence Gap in Numbers A 2026 survey found 87% of data leaders believed their data was AI-ready, while 43% simultaneously identified data readiness as their biggest barrier to extracting AI value. This gap reflects organizations' overconfidence in systems built for human consumers, not autonomous agents.

Five Attributes of AI-Ready Data

Each attribute addresses a job humans performed implicitly that now requires explicit engineering.

Trusted

Confidence built into data through accuracy, freshness, and validation—before agents access it.

Contextual

Business meaning (fiscal calendars, revenue definitions) moved from people's heads into the data layer.

Traceable

Complete reasoning chains reconstructable. Decisions captured as they happen, not explained after the fact.

Governed

Permissions scoped, controlled, and auditable by design—not bounded by role and judgment alone.

Operational

Data is not merely readable—it is actionable. Agents perform actions directly, not through manual intermediaries.

The AI-Ready Data Stack

The four topics—trust, context, access, and observability—aren't independent pillars. They form a dependency-ordered stack. You can't attach meaning to untrusted data. You can't safely let agents act without meaning to constrain them. Skip any foundational layer and everything above collapses. This is why many agentic AI programs stall: jumping to agent access without building the foundation.

Dependency Ordering Readiness is capped by the weakest foundational layer. A flawless context layer on untrusted data isn't agent-ready. Find your weakest row; invest there next.

Layer 1: Trusted Data Foundation

"Agents can't smell bad data."

Humans have an intuitive sense for data that looks wrong—numbers that seem off, dates that don't make sense, prices that appear too low. As Simon Willison notes, "language models are gullible," accepting and acting on whatever they receive. Errors don't trigger warnings; they cascade silently through agent workflows.

The Pricing Scenario

Worked Example: Stale Price Cascading
Product X price updates to $59.99 → Data source still shows $49.99 (stale) → Agent quotes customer $49.99 → $10 loss per unit, technically correct execution

Every step was technically correct. The data was wrong. No warning was raised.

Schema Is Law: Data Contracts as Code

Data contracts enforce schema as law rather than suggestion. While loose schemas merely inconvenience human analysts, they're dangerous for agents. A contract specifies strict logical types, quality rules (price must exceed zero), allowed values (currency in USD, EUR, GBP), and—critically—freshness SLAs defining maximum acceptable staleness.

Freshness SLAs: a subtle but critical point SLAs must be keyed to successful load completion, not when values last changed. A failed indexer makes data stale even when nothing appears changed. For unstructured data (RAG vector stores), measure when re-indexing completed, not when content was last modified.
Unstructured data needs contracts too Most agent data isn't tabular—it's documents, PDFs, and tickets chunked into vector stores for RAG. Contracts for unstructured data specify surrounding metadata: source and version identification, timestamp and access scope, rejection of empty or truncated chunks, detection of near-duplicate documents that skew retrieval, flagging failed extractions and OCR corruption, and monitoring embedding drift.

Enforcement happens along three dimensions: schema enforcement (types and constraints match), freshness SLAs (staleness within bounds), and quality gates (contracts validated in CI/CD, blocking deployment on failure).

Example: Open Data Contract Standard (YAML) 
apiVersion: v3.1.0
kind: DataContract
id: product-pricing
name: Product Pricing
version: 1.0.0
status: active
schema:
  - name: product_pricing
    physicalType: table
    properties:
      - name: product_id
        logicalType: string
        physicalType: varchar(64)
        required: true
        unique: true
        primaryKey: true
        primaryKeyPosition: 1
      - name: price
        logicalType: number
        physicalType: decimal
        required: true
        quality:
          - type: sql
            description: Every price must be greater than zero
            query: SELECT min({property}) FROM {object}
            mustBeGreaterThan: 0
      - name: currency
        logicalType: string
        physicalType: varchar(3)
        required: true
        quality:
          - type: sql
            description: Currency must be a supported ISO code
            query: |
              SELECT count(*) FROM {object}
              WHERE {property} NOT IN ('USD','EUR','GBP')
            mustBe: 0
      - name: ingested_at
        logicalType: timestamp
        physicalType: timestamp
        required: true
slaProperties:
  - property: latency
    value: 24
    unit: h
    element: product_pricing.ingested_at

The Quarantine Pattern

When data violates a contract, it must never reach agents. The quarantine pattern catches bad data before it enters agent-accessible storage.

In the pricing scenario, stale data violates the 24-hour freshness SLA, triggering quarantine. The agent responds "I don't have current pricing data" rather than confidently quoting wrong numbers.

Medallion Architecture

The medallion architecture organizes data through progressive quality tiers, with a new Adaptive Gold tier for agents.

Critical principle Agents access only Gold tier or above. Bronze and Silver remain for human investigation and debugging.

Confidence-Threshold Routing

Not all problematic data is clearly bad; some falls in gray areas. Confidence-threshold routing bridges full autonomy and human control. The agent assesses both model confidence and data-level quality signals (freshness, completeness, consistency). If confidence exceeds the threshold (e.g., 85%), it proceeds autonomously; below threshold, it defers to a human.

Antipattern: trusting model confidence alone Data quality signals should override model confidence. A model can be certain about stale data; freshness SLAs override that misplaced certainty. Start with hard gates: any contract or SLA breach forces human involvement regardless of other signals.

Where to start — Trust

  1. Define freshness SLAs for every dataset agents touch, varying per consumer (pricing tables fine on nightly batches for dashboards but need real-time updates for quoting agents)
  2. Implement quarantine gates validating contracts before agent-accessible storage, starting with highest-risk datasets (pricing, inventory, customer records)
  3. Use the Data Contract CLI, bringing contract governance into CI/CD as YAML definitions with automatic validation and deployment blocking
  4. Add confidence-threshold routing deferring to humans when quality signals drop below thresholds, starting conservatively around 90%

Layer 2: Traceability & Governance

With trusted data established, the harder question emerges: when regulators ask why an agent acted, can you explain? Traditional systems record what happened; agentic systems must explain why.

The Audit Gap

Worked Example: Letter of Credit

A bank's agent processes letter of credit LC-4892, approving a $2.4M transaction in 30 seconds.

KYC check → Sanctions screening → Credit terms evaluation → APPROVE (94% confidence)

Audit logs can show which tables were queried and when. They cannot explain why the agent checked sanctions before credit terms, or why it approved despite minor documentation gaps.

Agentic Lineage

Closing the audit gap requires agentic lineage—extending traditional data lineage to capture decision reasoning, using the traces-and-spans model from distributed systems observability.

Traditional Lineage

  • "Which sources were accessed?"
  • Records tables, timestamps, queries
  • Answers what happened

Agentic Lineage

  • "Why did the agent access X, because it found Y in source Z?"
  • Records traces with spans per step, reasoning and sources attached
  • Answers why—the complete decision pathway
The Regulatory Teeth Are Real The EU AI Act Article 12 mandates automatic logging sufficient to trace operation. Article 19 requires six-month retention. Penalties: up to €15M or 3% of global annual turnover. Tools: OpenTelemetry (Radar: Adopt), Langfuse (Trial), Arize Phoenix (Assess).

Staged Autonomy

Autonomy should be earned progressively, not granted wholesale. Just as you wouldn't give new employees unrestricted access immediately, don't grant agents full autonomy on day one.

Autonomy is staged; observability is not Autonomy progresses through four stages. Observability goes in from day one at full strength, regardless of autonomy level. Retrofitting observability onto a running system is painful—wire it before granting any autonomy, not after.

Security: The Lethal Trifecta

Three security patterns defend against Simon Willison's "lethal trifecta"—agents holding access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and external communication capability. A poisoned document could hijack such an agent via prompt injection and exfiltrate whatever it reaches.

Delegated Access

Agents act with the invoking user's permissions, not broad service accounts. "Alice's agent acting with Alice's permissions" is traceable; "the service account" is not.

Just-in-Time Credentials

Short-lived tokens scoped to specific tasks. Checking sanctions? Issue an OFAC-API-read token for that customer, valid five minutes. No standing credentials.

Least Privilege

Agents get minimum access their tasks require. Processing letters of credit doesn't need HR or marketing data reach. Shrink blast radius.

Where to start — Governance

  1. Instrument from day one. Every agent workflow should emit traces with spans for each step, including reasoning and sources. Use proven tools (OpenTelemetry, Langfuse, Arize Phoenix) rather than building custom solutions.
  2. Start in shadow mode. Lowest risk, highest learning. Agents recommend, humans decide. Build audit trails before compliance requires them; measure accuracy before granting autonomy.
  3. Implement delegated access. Agents inherit invoking user permissions; use just-in-time credentials with short expiry. No persistent tokens.
  4. Build for explainability. Whether regulators ask or not, audit trails answering "why" enable debugging, defense, and confidence to expand autonomy. Wire it in now; adding later is harder.

Layer 3: The Context Layer

"Your agent doesn't know what revenue means."

Ask an analyst "What was Q3 revenue for Product X?" and they know which tables to query, whether revenue means gross or net, how Q3 maps to your fiscal year. They accumulated this through institutional knowledge. Agents have none of it. Without context, they hallucinate answers or give up.

Nouns, Numbers, Verbs

Agents need three separate definition bodies, each serving a distinct purpose. Together they guarantee that context is declared once in version control rather than worked out afresh by models on every request.

Metrics as Code

Business logic lives in declarative definitions, not buried in BI tools or ad-hoc SQL views:

Example: dbt Semantic Model (YAML) 
semantic_models:
  - name: orders
    model: ref('orders')
    defaults:
      agg_time_dimension: order_date
    entities:
      - name: order_id
        type: primary
      - name: customer_id
        type: foreign
    dimensions:
      - name: order_date
        type: time
        type_params:
          time_granularity: day
    measures:
      - name: revenue
        agg: sum
        expr: order_amount - discount_amount
        create_metric: true

Same Question, Very Different SQL

The semantic model doesn't make agents smarter—it prevents guessing. For agents acting unchecked, that's what matters.

Without Semantic Model (agent guesses)
SELECT SUM(amount)
  FROM sales_data
 WHERE product = 'Product X'
   AND quarter = 'Q3'
With Semantic Model (constrained)
SELECT SUM(order_amount - discount_amount)
  FROM orders o
  JOIN products p
    ON o.product_id = p.id
 WHERE p.name = 'Product X'
   AND o.order_date
       BETWEEN '2025-07-01'
           AND '2025-09-30'

With the semantic model: agent sends a natural-language question → semantic model looks up metric definitions, valid dimensions, join paths, access rules via MCP → generates constrained SQL → warehouse executes → result flows back with full lineage metadata. Agents pick from governed metrics, never raw tables.

Impact: raw schema vs. semantic layer AtScale's text-to-SQL benchmark showed accuracy jumping from under 20% on raw schema to over 92.5% with a semantic layer on the same model. That's not a tuning improvement; it's the difference between guessing and being constrained.

Knowledge Graphs for Relationship Reasoning

Structured metric queries work well with tables, but some tasks require richer relationship reasoning across entities, events, and time over unknown depth—following chains until finding what you seek. That's the domain model's territory.

Microsoft's GraphRAG uses community detection for abstract queries traditional RAG can't handle. Graphiti builds temporally-aware knowledge graphs for evolving facts. The semantic model defines metrics; the graph carries entity connections over time. Together they give agents something approaching institutional memory.

Most implementations bootstrap from existing table structures, glossaries, and query patterns, letting humans curate on top rather than writing everything from scratch.

Where to start — Context

  1. Find conflicting metric definitions. Most organizations have competing definitions for key metrics (revenue being classic: gross vs. net, with/without returns). These conflicts are biggest risks and quickest wins.
  2. Pick a tool but focus on discipline. Any mainstream semantic layer tool works; what matters is keeping metric definitions in version control, ensuring one agreed definition per metric, routing agents through the layer.
  3. Route agents through the context layer. Agents see governed metrics and dimensions, never raw tables. MCP is common; dbt, Cube, and AtScale all ship MCP servers, but the principle—abstraction over raw schema—is what counts.
  4. Test adversarially. Every hallucination points to missing definitions. Fix the definition, not the prompt.

Layer 4: Agent-Ready Data Access

Once agents understand data and governance is in place, the question shifts: how do agents reach data and act on it? Traditional RAG only does step one—searching and retrieving content. It can't query live monitoring to check service status or create tickets in ServiceNow. That gap between searchable and actionable is this section's focus.

The Data Access Spectrum

Three tiers exist (per Microsoft's Cloud Adoption Framework for AI):

Three MCP Primitives

MCP (Model Context Protocol) rapidly became the default wiring mechanism. It defines three primitives on a risk gradient:

Resources (safest)

Read-only access to knowledge bases, configs, documentation. No state change. Maps to retrieval tier.

Prompts (moderate)

Shape agent behavior through instruction—decision trees, step-by-step triage. Influences reasoning without changing external state.

Tools (most dangerous)

Execute actions, modify state. Create tickets, issue refunds, update records. Maps to real-time query and write-back tiers. Requires strongest governance.

Design Capabilities, Not Endpoints

Antipattern: Naive API-to-MCP Conversion (TW Tech Radar: HOLD) Wrapping existing REST APIs one-to-one as tools creates tool sprawl. The agent chooses among 50 barely-distinguished tools; LLMs are bad at this; accuracy drops sharply as tool count climbs.
Design capabilities, not endpoints Expose functionality as a handful (5–10) of well-described business capabilities with rich descriptions and parameterized inputs. check_service_status(service, location) beats 50 per-service wrappers every time. This principle is protocol-agnostic—whether agents reach data through MCP or other standards, rich descriptions, parameterized access, and clear schemas make data agent-ready.

What a Capability Declares

Permissions & Owner

Who may invoke, acting as whom. Who's accountable when it misbehaves.

Preconditions

Conditions that must hold before action proceeds, checked against live state at decision time. A refund needs an original payment, not yet refunded, within authorization limits.

Reversibility Class

Cleanly reversible, reversible at cost (compensating transaction), or irreversible. A $50K internal ledger correction you can back out is safer to automate than a $200 external payment you can't claw back.

Key insight: Reversibility predicts safe autonomy better than transaction size A large but reversible internal operation is safer to automate than a small irreversible external one. Design guardrails around reversibility class, not dollar amounts.

Retrieved Text Informs, It Never Gates

Business documents hold rule definitions. But rules gating actions must not be read and interpreted at decision time. Rules are extracted ahead of time, curated, and stored as declared preconditions with links to source passages.

Informing (safe)

  • Retrieved text shapes proposals
  • Serves as evidence for human approvers
  • Influences what agents suggest
  • Document changes flagged for review

Gating (dangerous)

  • Retrieved text authorizes actions directly
  • Poisoned documents grant permissions
  • Injected text can fabricate evidence
  • Unknown cases trigger autonomous judgment

Declared rules—checked deterministically against live state—authorize actions. Agents may read unstructured content shaping proposals, but never act on retrieved text alone. Provenance links connecting declarations to source documents enable review when documents change—flagging derived rules for re-checking when sources move. Where declarations don't cover a situation, an undeclared case degrades the agent to supervised mode—it escalates rather than improvising from its own policy reading. This closes one of the three paths in the lethal trifecta: a poisoned document cannot grant the agent permission it didn't already have.

Where to start — Access

  1. Map data access tiers for your top three agent use cases. Classify whether each needs retrieval, real-time query, or write-back. Most gaps sit in real-time query and write-back.
  2. Design capabilities, not endpoints. Group existing APIs into 5–10 well-described business capabilities. Rich descriptions matter—they guide LLM tool selection.
  3. Start with MCP Resources. Read-only access is lowest-risk entry. Expose knowledge bases, configs, documentation as Resources. Graduate to Tools only with governance in place.
  4. Instrument from day one. Before deploying agents with write access, log every tool invocation: who triggered it, what was called, when, and critically, on whose behalf.

Data Ownership

Technology is necessary but insufficient. Every artifact in the stack—data contracts, metric definitions, access scopes, observability traces—produces something that must stay true. Artifacts don't maintain themselves.

The ownership principle The faster and more autonomous the consumers, the less you can afford ownerless data. When a contract blocks a deployment at 2 a.m., someone must be accountable. When finance and sales disagree on "revenue," someone owns the decision. When a new agent requests access, someone reviews the scope.

Where to Start

Four priorities, with observability running alongside everything else from day one.

1

Instrument from Day One

This runs alongside everything else, not sequentially. Observability is harder to retrofit than build in. You'll want audit trails answering "why" for debugging today and regulators tomorrow.

2

Contract Everything

Freshness SLAs, strict schema enforcement, quarantine for bad data. This is the floor everything stands on. Agents can't smell bad data; the architecture must.

3

Context Over Models

Once data is trusted, a context layer delivers highest returns. AtScale's benchmark: under 20% accuracy on raw schema → over 92.5% with a semantic layer, same model.

4

Read Before Write

Start with MCP Resources (read-only), graduate to Tools (write) only with governance. Earn autonomy in stages: shadow → supervised → guardrails → full.

The Bottom Line When agents become your primary data consumers, your data architecture becomes your AI architecture. The faster and more autonomous the consumers, the less you can afford ownerless data. Every dataset, contract, and metric needs a named owner, published contract and SLA, and versioned lifecycle—like APIs.
