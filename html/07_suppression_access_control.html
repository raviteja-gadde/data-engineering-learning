Suppression and Access Control

2026-08-23

Why Suppression Exists

Aggregated data can betray individual responses. The mechanism is simple arithmetic -- and it works with as few as three people.

The Arithmetic Deduction Attack

A team of 3 people reports scores on "I have a best friend at work" (1-5 scale).

The actual responses:

1 Alice
2 Bob
3 Carol

The published group mean is 2.0. Alice and Bob know their own answers.

Carol's score = (mean × count) - Alice - Bob = (2.0 × 3) - 1 - 2 = 3

Two people have deduced the exact response of their colleague. The "anonymous" survey is no longer anonymous.

This isn't theoretical. In a 3-person team, any two members can always reconstruct the third's answer from the group average. With 4 people, the math still constrains possibilities enough to make good guesses. With 10 people, the signal dissolves into noise.

This is why every serious survey platform suppresses results below a minimum group size. The principle applies far beyond surveys:

  • HIPAA -- healthcare data must suppress cell sizes below a threshold to prevent patient identification
  • FERPA -- educational records have similar suppression requirements
  • Bureau of Labor Statistics -- suppresses employment data for small geographic/industry cells
  • Census Bureau -- applies disclosure avoidance to any table where a cell represents too few respondents
The universal pattern When aggregate statistics are computed over small groups, the aggregation itself can be reversed to identify individuals. Suppression is the primary defense.

The Suppression Decision

Every time a report is rendered, each aggregate cell faces the same question: is the underlying group large enough to show safely?

Dynamic Suppression: Per Query, Not Per Row

The critical insight: suppression is a property of the query, not the data. The same underlying rows can pass or fail suppression depending on which dimensions appear in the GROUP BY.

Lab 01: Suppression Basics -- Same Data, Different Treatment

19 survey responses across 3 teams. Team A has 10 people (7 in New York, 3 in Austin), Team B has 3, Team C has 6 (4 in New York, 2 in Austin). Threshold = 4.

Cut 1: Group by Team

Team B (n=3) suppressed. Teams A and C pass.

SELECT team, COUNT(*) AS n,
       CASE WHEN COUNT(*) >= 4
            THEN ROUND(AVG(q01), 2) END AS avg_q01
FROM responses
GROUP BY team ORDER BY team
team | n | avg_q01 -------+----+-------- Team A | 10 | 4.0 Team B | 3 | NULL Team C | 6 | 3.5

Cut 2: Group by Team + Location

Same data, finer cut. Team A Austin (n=3) and Team C Austin (n=2) are now suppressed -- they passed at team level but fail at team+location.

team | location | n | avg_q01 -------+----------+---+-------- Team A | Austin | 3 | NULL Team A | New York | 7 | 4.0 Team B | Chicago | 3 | NULL Team C | Austin | 2 | NULL Team C | New York | 4 | 4.0

Cut 3: Group by Team + Location + Department

More dimensions = smaller cells = more suppression. In this dataset departments align with teams so counts don't change further, but in real data every added dimension fragments groups.

team | location | department | n | avg_q01 -------+----------+-------------+---+-------- Team A | Austin | Engineering | 3 | NULL Team A | New York | Engineering | 7 | 4.0 Team B | Chicago | Sales | 3 | NULL Team C | Austin | Product | 2 | NULL Team C | New York | Product | 4 | 4.0
Why you cannot pre-compute suppression You cannot stamp a "suppressed" flag on raw data. The same row passes at one GROUP BY level and fails at another. Suppression must be evaluated at query time based on the report's specific dimensions.

This is why suppression logic belongs in views, policies, or transformation layers -- not in application code that runs after the query. A dashboard with drill-down must re-evaluate suppression at each level.

k-Anonymity: The Formal Framework

Suppression is an informal version of a well-studied privacy concept called k-anonymity. A dataset satisfies k-anonymity if every combination of quasi-identifiers appears at least k times.

Quasi-Identifiers

Attributes that could narrow down who a record belongs to: team, location, department, tenure band, gender.

Sensitive Attribute

The thing being protected -- survey responses, scores, the data whose exposure causes harm.

When we say "suppress where count < 4," we are enforcing k-anonymity with k=4 on whatever grouping dimensions the report uses.

Two Ways to Satisfy k-Anonymity

Suppression

Hide the entire group's results. Replace with NULL or "insufficient data." The group exists but its aggregate is invisible.

Survey platforms typically use this approach.

Generalization

Roll up to a coarser grouping. Show department-level instead of team-level results. Trade granularity for privacy.

Data platforms may implement automatic roll-up.

Lab 02: k-Anonymity -- The Attacker Scenario

18 employees with quasi-identifiers (team, location, tenure_band) and a sensitive attribute (q01_score). An attacker knows their colleague is on Team C, in Austin, with 5+ years tenure.

Step 1: Find Vulnerable Groups

team | location | tenure | size | status -------+----------+-----------+------+----------- Team B | Chicago | 3-5 years | 1 | VULNERABLE Team B | Chicago | 0-2 years | 1 | VULNERABLE Team A | Austin | 3-5 years | 1 | VULNERABLE Team B | Chicago | 5+ years | 1 | VULNERABLE Team C | Austin | 5+ years | 1 | VULNERABLE Team A | New York | 0-2 years | 2 | VULNERABLE Team A | New York | 3-5 years | 3 | VULNERABLE

With k=4, every single group in this dataset is vulnerable. No combination has 4+ people.

Step 2: The Attack

SELECT team, location, tenure_band, q01_score
FROM employees
WHERE team = 'Team C' AND location = 'Austin'
  AND tenure_band = '5+ years'
team | location | tenure | score -------+----------+----------+------ Team C | Austin | 5+ years | 2 -- Exact identification!

Step 3: After Suppression (k=4)

team | location | tenure | score -------+----------+----------+------ Team C | Austin | 5+ years | NULL -- Attack blocked

Three Enforcement Layers

Suppression can be enforced at three levels, with dramatically different reliability:

The right answer Suppression is a data integrity concern, not a presentation concern. Enforce as close to the data as possible. Database views or transformation-layer models are correct. Application-level suppression is a last resort.

Enforcement Comparison

PropertyApplicationDatabase ViewTransform (dbt)
Reliability Low -- each consumer must implement High -- automatic for all High -- baked into output
Bypass risk High -- direct table access skips it Medium -- base table still queryable Low -- raw data not exposed
Auditability Scattered in app code Single SQL definition Version-controlled in git
Threshold change Update every consumer Update one view Update one macro
Dynamic cuts Flexible -- runtime Flexible -- GROUP BY Fixed at build time
Lab 05: Three Enforcement Approaches Side by Side

The same suppression rule (hide where n < 4) implemented at three levels.

Approach 1: Application-Level (Fragile)

results = db.query("SELECT team, AVG(score) FROM responses GROUP BY team")
for row in results:
    if row.count < 4:
        row.avg_score = None  # suppress in Python
Raw query exposes Team B's data: avg = 2.0 After suppress(): Team B avg = NULL Risk: A second dashboard that skips suppress() leaks the data.

Approach 2: Database View (Reliable)

CREATE VIEW team_scores_safe AS
SELECT team, COUNT(*) AS n,
       CASE WHEN COUNT(*) >= 4
            THEN ROUND(AVG(score), 2) END AS avg_score
FROM responses GROUP BY team;
All consumers query team_scores_safe: Team A | 10 | 4.0 Team B | 3 | NULL -- automatic Team C | 6 | 3.5

Approach 3: Transformation (dbt Pattern)

-- dbt macro: macros/suppress_below.sql
{% macro suppress_below(column, threshold=4) %}
    CASE WHEN COUNT(*) >= {{ threshold }} THEN {{ column }} END
{% endmacro %}

-- models/team_scores.sql
SELECT team,
       {{ suppress_below('ROUND(AVG(score), 2)') }} AS avg_score
FROM {{ ref('stg_responses') }}
GROUP BY team
Materialized output: Team A | 10 | 4.0 | suppressed=False Team B | 3 | NULL | suppressed=True Team C | 6 | 3.5 | suppressed=False

Row-Level Security (RLS)

Suppression controls what data is shown at all. Row-Level Security controls who sees which rows. Different problems, often needed together.

Without RLS, you have two bad options: separate tables per manager (doesn't scale) or application-level WHERE team = current_user_team on every query (one missed filter leaks all data). RLS moves the filter into the database engine itself.

PostgreSQL RLS Implementation

-- Enable RLS on the table
ALTER TABLE team_scores ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;

-- Policy: managers see only their team
CREATE POLICY manager_team_access ON team_scores
    FOR SELECT
    USING (manager_role = current_user);

-- Admin bypass: sees everything
CREATE POLICY admin_access ON team_scores
    FOR SELECT
    TO admin_role
    USING (true);

Snowflake Row Access Policies

CREATE ROW ACCESS POLICY team_visibility AS (team_name VARCHAR)
RETURNS BOOLEAN ->
    CASE
        WHEN IS_ROLE_IN_SESSION('ADMIN') THEN true
        WHEN team_name = CURRENT_ROLE() THEN true
        ELSE false
    END;

ALTER TABLE team_scores ADD ROW ACCESS POLICY team_visibility ON (team_name);

Three PostgreSQL roles (manager_a, manager_b, survey_admin) with RLS policies. All run the same SELECT.

Column Masking

Sometimes the row is visible but specific column values should be hidden based on the viewer's role. RLS hides rows; masking hides values within visible rows.

Use Cases

  • Manager sees team aggregates but not individual scores
  • HR analyst sees aggregates but not free-text comments
  • Admin sees everything

RLS vs Masking

  • RLS: row is entirely invisible
  • Masking: row visible, certain columns NULL
  • Both needed in multi-tenant analytics

PostgreSQL Masking Function

CREATE OR REPLACE FUNCTION mask_score(val NUMERIC)
RETURNS NUMERIC AS $$
BEGIN
    IF current_user = 'analyst_role' THEN
        RETURN val;    -- privileged: see actual score
    ELSE
        RETURN NULL;   -- everyone else: masked
    END IF;
END;
$$ LANGUAGE plpgsql;

-- Masked view wraps the raw table
CREATE VIEW team_scores_masked AS
SELECT team_name,
       mask_score(avg_q01)  AS avg_q01,
       mask_score(avg_q07)  AS avg_q07,
       response_count       -- count always visible
FROM team_scores_raw;

Snowflake Native Masking Policy

CREATE MASKING POLICY score_mask AS (val NUMERIC)
RETURNS NUMERIC ->
    CASE
        WHEN IS_ROLE_IN_SESSION('ANALYST') THEN val
        ELSE NULL
    END;

Two roles (analyst_role, viewer_role) query the same masked view.

-- As analyst_role (privileged): team_name | avg_q01 | avg_q07 | avg_q10 | response_count -----------+---------+---------+---------+--------------- Team Alpha | 4.20 | 3.80 | 3.10 | 10 Team Beta | 3.50 | 3.20 | 2.80 | 8 Team Gamma | 3.90 | 4.10 | 3.60 | 6 Team Delta | 2.80 | 2.50 | 2.10 | 3 -- As viewer_role (scores masked): team_name | avg_q01 | avg_q07 | avg_q10 | response_count -----------+---------+---------+---------+--------------- Team Alpha | NULL | NULL | NULL | 10 Team Beta | NULL | NULL | NULL | 8 Team Gamma | NULL | NULL | NULL | 6 Team Delta | NULL | NULL | NULL | 3

RLS + Suppression Together

In a real survey platform, you need both controls working simultaneously:

  1. RLS ensures Manager A cannot see Team B's data at all
  2. Suppression ensures that even for their own team, if a sub-group is too small, the aggregate is hidden
  3. Masking optionally hides specific column values based on the viewer's role

These are complementary, not alternative, controls. A complete access control system layers all three.

Policy-as-Code

When access control and suppression rules live in code (SQL, dbt macros, OPA policies), they become version-controlled, auditable, testable, and reviewable. Every change goes through pull requests like any other code.

Implementation Patterns

SQL Views/Policies

Most direct approach. Lives in the database. The CASE WHEN COUNT(*) >= 4 pattern enforced at the view level.

dbt Macros

{{ suppress_below(4) }} wraps the CASE WHEN pattern. One macro, one threshold, consistent across all models.

Open Policy Agent (OPA)

External policy engine. "Can user X see team Y's data?" is evaluated by OPA, query constructed accordingly.

ABAC

Attribute-Based Access Control. Policies reference user attributes (role, team) and data attributes (sensitivity level) to make access decisions.

The dbt Macro Pattern

-- macros/suppress_below.sql
{% macro suppress_below(column, threshold=4) %}
    CASE WHEN COUNT(*) >= {{ threshold }} THEN {{ column }} END
{% endmacro %}

-- models/team_scores.sql
SELECT team,
       {{ suppress_below('ROUND(AVG(score), 2)') }} AS avg_score
FROM {{ ref('stg_responses') }}
GROUP BY team
The CASE WHEN pattern This is the core SQL idiom for suppression. It appears everywhere -- in views, in dbt models, in ad-hoc queries. Know it cold: 
CASE WHEN COUNT(*) >= 4 THEN ROUND(AVG(score), 2) END AS avg_score
When the count is below threshold, CASE WHEN with no ELSE returns NULL automatically.

Industry Parallels

These patterns have decades of precedent across regulated industries:

HIPAA (Healthcare)

The Safe Harbor method requires suppression of cells with fewer than a threshold (often set at the "expert determination" level). Geographic data must be generalized to regions with 20,000+ population. Dates must be generalized to years for patients over 89.

Healthcare suppression patterns are the most mature and well-documented of any industry.

Bureau of Labor Statistics

BLS publishes employment statistics suppressed at multiple levels. If publishing county-level data would reveal a single employer's workforce, the entire county cell is suppressed.

They also apply complementary suppression: if suppressing one cell makes another cell calculable by subtraction from a published total, the second cell must also be suppressed. This is more sophisticated than simple threshold-based suppression.

FERPA (Education)

Student education records require suppression when cell sizes are small enough to identify individual students. Typical thresholds range from 5-10 depending on the institution and data type.

Census Bureau -- Differential Privacy

The Census Bureau's Disclosure Avoidance System is the most sophisticated suppression framework in existence. For the 2020 Census, they adopted differential privacy -- adding calibrated noise to published statistics rather than suppressing cells.

This is a more advanced approach than k-anonymity but solves the same fundamental problem: preventing individual identification from aggregate statistics.

Key Takeaways

  1. Suppression exists because small-group aggregates can be reversed to identify individual responses
  2. k-anonymity formalizes the minimum group size requirement -- survey "minimum N" is k-anonymity on report dimensions
  3. Suppression is per-query, not per-row. The same data passes or fails depending on the GROUP BY dimensions
  4. Enforce close to the data -- views, policies, transformation layer. Never rely solely on application code
  5. RLS and suppression solve different problems. RLS controls who sees which rows; suppression controls whether an aggregate is safe to show. Both are needed
  6. Column masking adds a third layer: visible rows with hidden values based on role
  7. Policy-as-code (SQL policies, dbt macros, OPA) makes access rules auditable, testable, and version-controlled
